# Scam-Proofing Your Little Shop

Most transactions are honest. The scammy ones share habits, and once you
know the habits they're almost boring to spot. This chapter is your
short list — for buyers *and* for an inbox that occasionally pretends to
be eBay.

## Red flags in messages from "buyers"

A message contains a scam characteristic when it includes any of these:

- **Overpayment.** "I'll send you $70 for the $25 item, keep the rest for
  your trouble." The overage has ways of vanishing after you've refunded
  real money. Decline every time.
- **Off-platform payment.** "Venmo me / Zelle me / wire me and I'll pay
  no fees." Every part of this sentence is the scam: outside the
  marketplace you have **no buyer-protection shield, no fee math, and no
  one to appeal to**.
- **Speed pressure with specifics.** "My son arrives at 4pm and will pick
  it up in person; here's a check for shipping." A hurry dramatized
  with details is pressure wearing a costume.
- **Codes, links, and "verify" requests.** Anyone who asks for a code
  texted to your phone is trying to log in *as you*. Codes are never
  shareable. Nothing legitimate requires one.
- **"Just ship it any way you want" with pre-printed labels.** A real
  label is real; a label that reroutes to some third address is a
  laundering route, not postage.
- **Emotional story + lining-up a quick price cut.** We can all be kind
  *and* still say "everything's through the marketplace, sorry."

The correct one-line reply to nearly all of them, calm and final:

> "Thanks for your interest — I only sell and ship through the
> marketplace, so everything's covered for both of us."

That sentence doesn't argue. It doesn't have to.

## The inbox that isn't eBay

Phishing emails are just fake-buyer messages wearing a uniform. Checks:

- **Real platforms never ask** for your password, a code, a card number,
  or your Social Security number by email. Assume urgent = fake.
- **Don't click; go.** If an email says your account has a problem, open
  a *new* tab, type the marketplace address yourself, and look. If the
  problem is real, it'll be visible from there. This one habit ends 90%
  of phishing.
- **The message shows up *in the app*** vs. only by email: platform
  messages relating to real orders appear under "Messages"/"My Account".
  A payment "confirmation" that arrives *only* as an email — with a
  hopeful-sounding file to download — is the classic fake. Real payment
  appears in your actual balance. **Balance is truth; email is suggestion.**

## When you've already shipped and the story turns

Two recurring shapes, and your shield for both:

- **"Item not received."** Tracking is your whole answer — which is why
  buying labels in-app (they auto-upload tracking) is both a convenience
  and a legal-looking paper trail. If tracking shows delivered but the
  buyer says no, the marketplace's protection process will review it
  calmly. Reply once, attach your [pack photo and weight
  note](04-shipping-without-stress.md), and stop typing; escalate within
  the platform's own process — it exists, it's staffed, and it beats
  improvising your own remedies.
- **"Item not as described."** Check whether page photos really showed
  what arrived (they did, or you'd have mentioned it first — see the
  [describing chapter](03-photos-and-listings.md)). Either way, the
  part-time play is usually: accept the return, refund when it lands.
  Not out of guilt — out of *arithmetic*, since your review score is
  worth more than a $15 argument. If the return is slow, the platform
  intervenes for you; you don't have to improvise remedies.

## A scam test that fits on an index card

Before you reply to *any* unusual message, run four questions:

1. Am I being asked to move money, codes, or the conversation **off the
   platform**? (If yes: no.)
2. Is there a **hurry with a story** attached? (If yes: slow down.)
3. Does the transaction leave me with **no platform protection**?
   (If yes: stop.)
4. If I ignore this message entirely, would a real buyer simply *buy
   through the listing*, like the last twenty honest people did?
   (If yes: that's your answer.)

If all four come back clean, respond through the platform normally. That
four-card test — plus "balance is truth, email is suggestion" — covers
most of the scam-shaped things you'll meet in a part-time resale life.

## What's *not* a scam (so you don't get paranoid)

- A buyer asking a normal question twice, because texts interrupt lives.
- A buyer sending an offer 20% lower — offers are shopping, not fraud.
- A buyer who seems new with no feedback; everyone started at zero
  (protections still apply to you both).
- A return that actually arrives in more worn shape than it left —
  painful, occasionally unfair, but not a scam. Deal with it calmly via
  the platform.

If you'd like a printable version of the four questions plus the
calm-reply line, the [starter checklist](starter-checklist.md) includes
the seller's side, and this chapter is the why behind it. When a specific
message makes you nervous *and* the test is ambiguous, the rule of the
whole chapter is: **do nothing for one day.** Real deals keep almost
always; fake ones evaporate.
