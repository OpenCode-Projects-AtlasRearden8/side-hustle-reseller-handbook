Fees and Taxes (Plain Round Numbers)

This is the chapter people skip and then regret at two in the morning. Here's the friendly version, with numbers you can do in your head, and a clear flag every time a number is a rounded guess rather than gospel.

Where the money goes on one sale

Sold for $20.00 — here's the pipeline, in round numbers:

StageRounded numberWhat it is
Buyer pays you$20.00Including any shipping they paid
Marketplace fee− $2.60eBay's final value fee — roughly 13% plus a small per-order amount
Payment processingoften includedThe "roughly 13%" above covers most of it on eBay; other platforms quote similar
Shipping label (if you paid)− $4.50What your scale-and-box routine chose
Your out-of-pocket item cost− $1.00Rarely worth arguing about at these levels
Your money before tax= about $11.90This is the number that matters

The big takeaway, in round numbers: the marketplace keeps about 13-15% of everything. When something sells for $30, mentally subtract 15 first, and then deal with the shape of what's left.

The Half Rule (a quick sanity check)

Here's the trick that keeps a part-timer from chasing bad numbers: for a typical book-or-small-item sale —

You keep about half, minus your cost.

Twenty dollars sold → about $10 in your hand before tax. Ten dollars sold → about $5. If someone at a yard sale asks "How much do these sell for?" you can answer in round numbers before opening a calculator:

It listed aroundYou'd keep (before your cost)
$10about $5
$20about $10
$50about $25
$100about $50 (slightly more, fees shrink percent-wise, but round is round)

The Half Rule is deliberately crude. When a decision is worth real math — bundles, whole stash, anything over about $40 — open the Fee-think worksheet, where the arithmetic is one subtraction per line. For anything exact and high-volume, the companion Resale Lot Builder tool forecasts profit per lot with fee rates you set yourself.

Amazon, in one paragraph

Amazon's fee math is famously stackier than eBay's: referral fees vary by category (a rounded rule: many categories are around 8-15%, books about 15%), and if you use their fulfillment service you'll also pay outbound fulfillment fees per unit (which vary by item size as well as weight). Third-party new-item rules and category gating also play a role. Round conclusion for a beginner: the same item often nets you noticeably less on Amazon than the Half Rule suggests — which is exactly why this book told you to start on eBay-adjacent categories and treat Amazon as a second language you add later. (Compare exact rates for one item across marketplaces any time with the companion Shipping-Cost Comparer for the shipping side of Amazon's math.)

Sales tax, in plain round numbers (the part that surprises people)

Two different taxes are in play, and you should never confuse them:

  1. **Sales tax — the one buyers pay.** The round-number comfort of the year: eBay, Amazon, and similar large marketplaces **collect and remit sales tax for you** in most U.S. states, because states require them to. Buyers see tax added at checkout; it never lands in your balance; you do nothing. Caveat in plain terms: the rule is yours to confirm for your state, and "my own local in-person sale" (like... a yard sale) works differently. When in doubt, ask a tax professional a one-time, one-question version of the question.
  2. **Income tax — the one you pay. Marketplace fees and shipping you paid are business expenses**, not profit; the taxable number is:

> sale price − item cost − fees − shipping = profit ← the taxable > word is profit, not sale.

If you sold $300 worth and spent $120 getting the stuff and $90 in fees and shipping, your taxable profit is about $90, not $300. People who forget this over-pay. People who remember it sleep.

How much to set aside (the envelope method)

Round-number guidance: if you expect to owe income tax in any form, keep about a quarter — call it 25% of profit — untouched as a tax cushion.

Concretely: profit in, envelope-add. When profit for the month is $50, spend what you like and about $12 goes into an envelope that says TAXES. If at tax time the envelope is bigger than the bill, the change is yours — a nice surprise, mattering for nothing but morale. If the envelope is short, it did its quiet, boring job of making the shortfall small.

If reselling stays genuinely small (profitably hobby-level: a few hundred dollars of profit a year), the whole tax business usually fits on the regular tax form as a small line item. When it grows into a business — or if you start a genuine LLC or hire anyone — that's the moment a real accountant is worth their hourly rate. This book is education, not advice; the envelope is a habit, the accountant is the expert.

Estimated-tax quarter notes, briefly: U.S. tax law asks some side-income earners to file estimated tax payments quarterly, but the thresholds and mechanics are specific. Round-number version: until your sideline profit is meaningfully large (numbers like "a couple thousand dollars for the year" territory), most beginners simply land inside the safe-harbor range and owe it all at annual filing. As always when the number stops being small: professional, not blog post.

Danger at the top: the one fee you forget

The fee nobody predicts is the fee on the shipping you charged. eBay (and similar platforms) charges the ~13% cut on the total the buyer paid, including shipping. So if you listed "$18 free shipping" and packed a box whose label costs $9, do the Half Rule on the whole $18 — that free shipping isn't free to you. Listings with calculated shipping typically pass the postage through, but the fee still rides the shipping amount. This is the reason the fee-think worksheet has a line called "buyer paid for shipping (fee applies)" — it is the number beginners forget three times and remember forever after.

Five numbers worth writing on a sticky note (until they become instinct)

  1. 13-15% — the marketplace cut, roughly (eBay's rounded average).
  2. Half. — your rough keep, on small items, before cost, and *before tax*.
  3. 25% of profit — the tax-envelope rule.
  4. Sale − cost − fees − shipping = profit — the only formula you must memorize.
  5. Free shipping isn't free — the fee rides the whole buyer payment.
