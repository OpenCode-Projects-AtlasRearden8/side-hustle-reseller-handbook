Finding Inventory (Sourcing Calmly)

Sourcing giddiness is the #1 beginner budget-killer. Nearly everyone's first month includes one purchase they think of as "the twelve mugs incident." This chapter exists so yours is small.

Where beginners should look first — in this order

  1. Your own home. Genuinely. Closets, garage, kitchen shelf of gadgets, the stack of books you swore you'd reread. Total cost: $0, and your records chapter starts with an *item cost of zero* — which is legal, honest, and a lovely first practice.
  2. Thrift stores. Fixed prices, no haggling, usually good return lines. Pattern: pick one store, same day each week, forty minutes, walk the two sections that match your chosen categories, leave.
  3. Library book sales. Often $1–2 per book on the last day, sometimes "fill a bag for $5" days. Bring a bag that isn't embarrassing to fill.
  4. Garage and yard sales. Cheapest, most time-consuming, requires small talk. Bring exact cash and a polite no. "Would you take three dollars?" is a complete sentence on both sides.
  5. Clearance aisles and store closeouts. Perfectly legal and common. Keep receipts in a folder or an app's camera roll — they're your cost records later.

Skip for now: estate sales (emotional pricing), storage auctions (a whole sub-hobby), and anything requiring a paid membership. You can add those later, after you know your own pace.

The two rules that keep sourcing calm

The spending rule. Decide your sourcing budget before you walk in — say, $20 per trip — and pay cash if you can. When it's gone, the trip is done. A filled budget doesn't mean you'll fill a shelf; it means you've bought things you can explain at tax time without a headache. Anything you can't trace is inventory you can't price honestly.

The 15-minute rule. If you're standing in a store unsure about an item, give yourself 15 minutes of Hey-let-me-think, or set the item down and finish shopping, then decide at the register. Small items can be undervalued or overvalued; the feeling of "this is rare" is not research. Look up sold prices on your phone before the register (see photos and listings for the full pricing method: sold listings, not asking prices).

A hard-earned truth: "I've never seen one before" is not the same as "this is valuable." The truth is usually "this is worthless or worth more than you realize, and only sold listings can tell you which."

Walking away (a required skill)

You will pick up items that seem interesting and turn out to be unsellable-to-you for reasons you only learn by inspecting: missing pieces, sticky residue, hidden stains, a hairline crack, the wrong edition, an inventory tag buried deeper than your patience. Put it back, every time, with no speech act. The item you don't buy is a shipment you never have to think about again.

Some categories have a walk-away reflex worth building early:

  • Anything explaining it "just needs a small part" — you're the one who has to find that part.
  • Anything you own zero expertise on but "goes for hundreds online" — see the scam chapter for the marketplace version of that sentence aimed at you, later.
  • Anything whose box weighs more than the product. Shipping math punishes packaging that out-masses its content.

The burnout budget (spend the money, not your weekend)

Budget rules protect your money. A calendar rule protects your energy. Both exist in the same workbook:

  • One sourcing trip per week, four hours total, day of week fixed. If a week is busy, skip it — supply will wait for you.
  • A "we don't buy that here" list. For example: "at thrift stores, we buy books and one other category, never fragile glass, never anything over $15 without a phone check first."

Keep your sourcing notes with your listing notes. A simple one-line pattern works: "Sat 10/26: Goodwill East, $17.40 spent — 6 books, 1 tool. Found: nothing over $5, good day." That sentence, saved 40 times a year, is half your bookkeeping already done (the records chapter shows the whole cut-down version).

Your first-month sourcing scorecard

You are doing it right if:

  • Your sourcing budget was spent on items you can list within a week — not "someday" research boxes.
  • At least 20% of the money went to items sourced free from your own house.
  • You put back at least three things you wanted.
  • You never once bought something you couldn't name the four-question test answers for.

And if you break a rule and buy the twelve mugs anyway? Welcome back — the fee-think worksheet will tell you what they really cost, and the quitting-politely chapter can even turn leftover inventory into a tidy exit when the time comes.
