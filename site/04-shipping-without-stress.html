Shipping Without Stress

Nothing about reselling stays fun once shipping becomes a daily emergency. The fix isn't willpower — it's three boring supplies, one honest scale, and a drop-off routine you never have to renegotiate. (This chapter is about judgment: when, what, how, and how to stay calm. When you want a computer to compare exact carrier rates for a given weight and box, that's what the companion Shipping-Cost Comparer CLI tool is for.)

The shipping station: one tray, ten dollars

Pick one place — a corner of a table, a shelf, one drawer — that holds only:

  1. A kitchen scale that reads in grams and pounds-ounces. The single most stress-killing purchase in this whole book, usually $10–15. Weigh your item with its packaging, in the box or envelope you'll mail it in, packed.
  2. Tape — real packing tape, not decorative. One big roll lasts months.
  3. Boxes in three sizes and padded envelopes in two sizes. Build the bank for free: flatten boxes from your own shopping, grab the odd clean box from a store's take-one stack, and save padded envelopes that arrive at your door in good shape. Never reuse a box that still shows a barcode label from a different carrier — peel or black it out first.

Optional but beautiful: a puppy pad or small towel under the station, so a dented can of beans rolling away is a laugh, not a mess. When the station is tidy, shipping is a chore; when it's chaotic, shipping is a drama. Choose chore.

The one rule of shipping weight

The scale decides, not the guess. Carriers price by weight and dimensions, and rounding up one size class can double your label price. Weigh everything packed: item, box, filler, tape, the little thank-you note if you include one. Write the packed weight on a scrap or in a note. This number — not a memory of "eh, felt like a pound" — is what you use when you buy the label, and at drop-off the counter never corrects you.

Shipping-station math that pays for itself: a $12 scale that saves you $2 per misjudged guess pays for itself after your sixth package. Few purchases in this whole book have better math.

Wrapper wisdom (short version)

  • Padded envelopes are for anything soft, light, or flat: books, video games, folded jeans, small boxed items without corners to dent. They're the cheapest protection-per-dollar you can source.
  • Small product boxes from your own shopping (with the label-covered sides taped over) are free, and free is a price that can't be improved.
  • Bubble wrap and paper filler for anything rigid or valuable. Not required for a video game in a sleeve; required for anything fragile over the "I wouldn't cry over it" line.
  • **No liquids, no batteries, nothing against hazmat rules until you've read those rules** — books and jeans first forever.

Buying the label: where the calm lives

  1. Buy the label through the marketplace (eBay and Amazon both offer discounted label rates when you buy in-app). It's usually far cheaper than the counter, it auto-fills the buyer's address — a typo firewall is worth more than any loyalty program — and it uploads tracking automatically, which keeps you protected when scam-proofing questions come up.
  2. Let the label tool choose the service class. When you buy a label in-app, it suggests a service based on the packed weight you entered and the box dimensions. Don't memorize service names on day one; read the suggested options in plain order, and pick the cheapest one that isn't a multi-week trip. If your listing charged the buyer for shipping, your test is one line long: the label price must not be more than what the buyer paid — and that comparison belongs to the fee-think worksheet, which keeps one row for exactly that.
  3. Tape the label flat, all four edges, on the biggest flat side. If using paper, don't let tape cover the barcode itself.

Insurance is built into many in-app label services up to a threshold (about $100 of coverage on eBay's standard labels, as a plainly rounded figure). One rule of thumb for a part-timer: automatic coverage handles most everyday items. For anything you'd be sad to lose, the box-packing discipline from this chapter matters way more than the insurance upgrade. Careful packing prevents the claim you never need to file.

The drop-off routine

  • One drop-off day per week (many sellers pick the same day their sourcing trip falls, or the day after). Batch errands, save gas, save brain.
  • Know your two nearest drop-off points: the post office lobby (usually the 24-hour self-serve wall) and one UPS-type store. Two points is enough.
  • Sunday-evening or drop-off-morning ritual: every sold-and-shipped-by tomorrow item is wrapped, taped, labeled, stacked, and photographed before the label scan. Yes — **photograph heavy fragile boxes next to the scale** showing the weight and any addresses before you let go of them. It takes ten seconds and it's the evidence when things go missing (proof-of-shipment discipline overlaps heavily with the scam-proofing checklist).

The truth about "it got there broken"

It happens to everyone eventually. What keeps it rare:

  • Fill the space. A pressed-lid box that goes thunk when shaken is the enemy. If it moves, wrap it more.
  • Corners and screens are where damage reports come home. Extra wrap there, always.
  • If the buyer reports damage: refund fast and apologize, don't investigate like an insurance adjuster. What we're protecting — reviews, sanity, part-time pace — is worth far more than one item's value. Save the photos and weight-note for the eventual carrier claim, not for arguing.

Shipping, in six sentences (the chapter, shortened)

  1. Weigh everything packed, on a real scale, before you buy a label.
  2. Pick the box to fit the item, not your aspirations.
  3. Buy the discounted label in-app and let it handle the details.
  4. Tape flat over the edges, keep the barcode showing.
  5. Drop off on your standing day, after the ten-second pack photo.
  6. Fill empty box space, then fill it again.

When that rhythm runs without thinking, on a part-time clock, ask the five-question fun check later — and in the meantime, go be proud of the box drawer.
