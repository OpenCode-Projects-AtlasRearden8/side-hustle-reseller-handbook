Photos and Listings (Just Your Phone)

Good cell-phone photos aren't an accessory to selling — on most marketplaces they are the listing. This chapter covers the whole loop: shoot, describe, price, publish — then batch it so ten items take ninety minutes, not one.

The five-minute photo setup

You need one window with daylight, not a lamp:

  1. Background. A plain white or light-gray surface: a poster board, a cookie sheet, a white pillowcase. Not your patterned bedsheets. The plain background does two jobs — it makes the item readable, and it quietly signals "careful seller."
  2. Light. Daylight from a window pointing at the item, never from behind it. If you have no window free, a porch or car hood in shade works. No flashlight-on-the-dinner-table telling fibs about color.
  3. The 8 photos. Shoot these, always, in this order:
  • front, straight-on
  • back
  • both sides
  • any flaw (stain, scuff, missing something) — big and in focus
  • the label / brand / size tag
  • inside / what's included, everything on the surface
  • one photo of it being held or measured, for scale

That's the whole shot list. Items under $20 can honestly do with 5, and books with 4. Nothing gets zero flaw photos — see the honesty section below.

Describing like a friend would

Write the description the way you'd text a friend who asked "what's wrong with it?" You are not writing persuasive ad copy; you're writing an honest report card:

Mission-style oak bookends, pair. Heavy — just under 4 lb for both.
One scuff on the left base (photo 5), felt bottoms intact.
All four felt pads present. Measures 6" x 3.25" x 4".
From a clean smoke-free, paw-free home. Ships USPS Ground Advantage.

Three rules inside that sample:

  • Flaws before features is optional, but *flaws before the buyer finds them* is not. A buyer who's already seen the scuff in photo 5 doesn't feel surprised.
  • Measure with a tape, write the numbers plainly. "Compact" is a feeling; "6 in" is a fact.
  • Say how it ships. "Ships USPS Ground Advantage" tells the buyer what to expect and keeps your shipping chapter promises consistent.

Titles that search engines can help

Most marketplaces are search boxes before they're shops. Titles obey one simple law: be findable, then be honest, in under 80 characters.

  • All caps and exclamation marks help nobody find you.
  • The words a buyer would type: brand, item, key attribute ("The Great British Baking Show hardcover cookbook 2019" not "STUNNING MUST-SEE RARE CATALOG").
  • Skip vanity words in the title; "rare" belongs in spare punctuation.

Item specifics (eBay calls them that; Amazon calls most of them required) matter more than beauty in the title: brand, model, size, color, condition. Fill them conscientiously — they power the filters buyers hide behind.

Pricing: sold listings, not hopes

Before you list, look up what actually sold for the same item or the closest thing to it:

  1. Search the item name on the marketplace.
  2. Filter to sold/completed listings — on eBay that's the "Sold Items" filter. Asking prices are wishes; sold prices are facts.
  3. Take the middle of the recent solds, not the top. The one $75 sale three years ago is not your price; the eight sales this month are.
  4. Round to a clean number. Clean numbers are easier to remember on reprice day.

If there are no recent solds, price for the walk-away rule: a price you'd still be fine with when three weeks of silence follow. When the arithmetic gets real — bundles, cost, fees — that's what the Fee-think worksheet is for, and its numbers are designed for the back of an envelope.

Honor the markdown clock

Listings age. Pick a personal policy, write it down, and apply it without much thought:

  • Days 0–14: your researched price.
  • Day 15: knock about 10–15% off (round number: reduce by a round dollar amount).
  • Day 45: drop to your walk-away floor, or relist to refresh.
  • Day 90–120: consider the honest endgame — donate it, bundle it (the companion Resale Lot Builder tool forecasts whether bundling beats selling separately), or let it go.

That schedule is a template, not a law. The point is having one, so repricing is a chore with a calendar, not a mood.

Batch it: your listing rhythm

Listing one item takes ten times the energy of listing five in a row, so batch:

  1. Shoot a whole batch of 5–10 items in one five-minute setup (same background, same table).
  2. Draft them all, then publish. Drafting with no pressure, then hitting publish ten times in a row, is a different feeling than starting from a blank form every five minutes.
  3. End the session by writing down the batch in your own record — one line per item, exactly as the records chapter prescribes.

When you photograph ten items on the same table in one sitting, compare against this chapter's shot list — the last three items get the "did I get the label?" double-check. Fatigue photos are blurry; nobody's scolded, we just take the second shot.

That's the whole loop. When you finish your first ten listings, treat it like a milestone; the starter checklist treats it like one too.
